1 (from Pfleeger, "8.7 Exercises"): How do cloud threats differ from traditional threats?  
Against what threats are cloud services typically more effective than local ones?

Traditional threats are always in your physical environment, whereas cloud threats are in the cloud provider’s environment. Most cloud services are accessed on a public-facing platform, which makes them more accessible to hackers via the internet. Instead of threats being mitigated solely by your internal cybersecurity team, cloud threats must be mitigated through collaboration between your internal team and the cloud provider. The security benefits of using cloud services include automated updates, since security patches are handled by the cloud provider. This ensures updates are automatically enforced, rather than relying on internal teams to manually update on-prem systems. In the cloud space, the use of APIs increases the number of threats because integrating different cloud components and third-party tools and software—such as GitHub—creates additional attack surfaces. If not configured correctly, these links open the door to eavesdropping or data exfiltration. Adopting cloud services can decrease the amount of physical security that an organization is responsible for, since large on-prem data centers are no longer required. In a hybrid model, the organization maintains a smaller physical footprint, which reduces the number of access points that must be secured. If a copy of on-premises data is stored in the cloud, this also provides an additional layer of protection against distributed denial-of-service attacks and improves overall availability.

2 (also from Pfleeger)

How do cloud services make Data Loss Prevention more difficult?  How can customers 
wishing to enforce DLP mitigate this issue?

Cloud services make Data Loss Prevention (DLP) more difficult because users are no longer working strictly within their company’s internal environment, which they are most familiar with. There is often a learning curve when using cloud environments, especially with low-code products that offer many configuration options. This learning curve increases the risk of misconfiguration and makes data loss prevention more challenging—particularly in large technology companies like Qualcomm, where many teams work on different projects. Engineers may be highly specialized in their fields but may not be experts in cloud services. While they may understand that integrating a cloud setup into their project would be beneficial, they may not fully understand how to implement it securely. 

To mitigate this issue, companies should have an internal point of contact who is highly knowledgeable in cloud security or a dedicated cloud expert who can serve as an internal consultant for engineers integrating cloud services into their projects. This expert should guide teams on how to properly set up their cloud environments and securely link them to the internal network. The priority should always be enforcing the principle of least privilege for users and even between services within the cloud environment that may be public-facing by default.

There should also be internal documentation and standardized guides detailing secure configurations from past projects so that teams do not have to reinvent the wheel and can instead reference proven implementations. Additionally, organizations should adopt cloud-native DLP tools to help protect sensitive assets. For example, if large volumes of data are transferred, these tools should generate alerts to ensure that the activity was intentional and properly authorized.

4 You are in charge of security for a small rocket company.  Your company takes on a 
number of engineering design contracts each lasting around 6 months, along with some
 long-term work for various aerospace customers.  It is sometimes difficult to get 
 all the resources (CPU and storage) needed.  You are asked to give the benefits and 
 drawbacks of moving your 3 servers and storage to the cloud.   Give a bulleted list
  of drawbacks and benefits and make a recommendation.

Drawbacks of moving 3 servers and storage to the cloud:

Larger attack surface for top secret (government contracts) and extremely sensitive intellectual property.

Need to hire a cloud consultant or a cloud expert so that we can ensure we configure the cloud servers and cloud storage properly, and so that we can continue to be compliant with auditors such as NIST that allow us to maintain our secret and top secret labs where we work on our government contracts.

Dependent on internet connectivity.

Benefits of moving 3 servers and storage to the cloud:

Easily scalable; instantly acquire access to resources needed, especially for short-term projects.

Data protection since storage is off-prem; data can be recovered quickly if a disaster were to take place.

Hands-off maintenance for security updates on servers.

Recommendation:
I recommend we invest in a hybrid setup so that we can balance compliance with scalability. The three servers being moved to the cloud should be our least sensitive, non-classified systems, while internal classified workloads remain on-premises. The cloud storage environment should hold all standard project data, but any sensitive or classified data stored there should exist only as a backup copy as part of our disaster recovery plan.

Access to sensitive backups should be heavily restricted and limited to personnel responsible for data recovery, who should clearly be identified on an access list. The cloud environment should not have direct access to sensitive on-premises systems. Instead, backup updates should occur through a single, controlled on-premises access point, which is wiped or disconnected after each update. This ensures that sensitive systems remain isolated while still benefiting from cloud-based redundancy and recovery capability.


5  Your boss says to you, "You keep saying that the cloud will increase our attack 
surface.  I don't understand.  Explain to me what an "attack surface" is and why
 our use of the cloud will increase it.  Give me some examples."

Cloud technology allows us to host assets such as servers, virtual machines (VMs), databases, and containers using the provider’s physical computing power. Cloud service providers grant us remote access to those assets through their platforms. Since the cloud service providers manage the hardware required for these virtual assets, there is still a possibility that those assets can be attacked. An attack surface includes any vector that an attacker can attempt to exploit. It is vital to choose a reputable cloud service provider that enforces strong physical security, secure configurations, constant updates, continuous monitoring, and mitigation of security vulnerabilities needed to run their services.

Our current attack surface is limited to the physical assets that we directly manage and update. Cloud services increase our attack surface because we must also account for another environment that we do not physically manage. Some of the risks include sensitive data being stored externally (in some cases in a shared virtual environment), new attack vectors created by linking the cloud service to our company’s infrastructure, and the risk of employees of cloud service providers being targeted in phishing campaigns.

This is why service level agreements (SLAs) are vital to ensure our environment and data are protected by the cloud service providers. Some companies even purchase cybersecurity insurance in the case of a breach or attack. As developers using cloud services, it is important to properly configure APIs and Identity and Access Management (IAM) across all cloud products in the environment, and to ensure that only the necessary ports are open.
